# University Student Mental Stress Pattern Analysis Using Unsupervised Learning

**Course:** CSE 4112 — Machine Learning Laboratory
**Institution:** Khulna University of Engineering & Technology (KUET)
**Date:** August 2026

---

## Abstract

We collected 987 survey responses from Bangladeshi engineering students measuring academic stress
across twelve areas, then used unsupervised learning to find groups of students who experience
stress *differently* — not simply more or less of it. After preprocessing and factor analysis, we
clustered the data using k-means, Expectation-Maximisation, hierarchical and spectral clustering,
and compared the results in both Python and WEKA. Three student personas emerged, and a decision
tree was used to express each one as a readable rule. The groups are best described as a useful
segmentation of a continuum rather than sharply separated populations, and this report states that
plainly.

## 0. Related works

Six sources shaped our design: two reference datasets, held in
[`related_works/`](related_works/), and four published studies — two that profile student stress
with the same family of methods we use, and two that set the standard against which we judge our
own result. Full citations are collected in [§11](#11-references).

### 0.1 Chusniyah et al. (2020) — multi-country student mental health dataset

> Chusniyah, T., Jaafar, J. L. S., Chaiwutikornwanich, A., Kuswandi, D., Firmanto, A., Mustopa, A.,
> & Zahra, G. A. (2020). *Dataset on positive mental health of Indonesian, Malaysian, and Thailand
> university students.* **Data in Brief, 32,** 106314. doi:10.1016/j.dib.2020.106314

A cross-national survey dataset on university student mental health. **What we took from it:**
the practice of publishing the questionnaire alongside the data, and the convention of reporting
descriptive statistics for every item before any modelling. It confirmed that self-report Likert
instruments are the standard approach in this problem area.

### 0.2 Hussen, Ibrahim & Nafea (2026) — student psychological stress survey dataset

> Hussen, F. M., Ibrahim, H. M., & Nafea, A. A. (2026). *Student Psychological Stress Survey
> Dataset (University of Anbar, Iraq)* [Data set], Version 2. Mendeley Data.
> doi:10.17632/428whwsk6c.2

A stress survey of **610 students** at the University of Anbar, Iraq, with **15 Likert items**
(5-point verbal scale: Never … Always) covering academic, financial, social and emotional
dimensions, plus Gender, Age, Marital Status and Stage of study. It is distributed in three
versions — **Raw**, **Encoded** and **Clustering** — where the clustering version adds a
`Stress_Class` label with **three classes** (241 / 210 / 159), generated by running **k-means on
the encoded items**.

**What we took from it:**

- The raw → encoded → clustering-ready file progression, which we mirror in our own outputs.
- Its item pool overlaps ours on exam anxiety, sleep difficulty, financial pressure and
  student–teacher relations, which supported our item selection.
- **The closest thing we have to a published baseline for the approach we argue against.** Their
  three classes come from k-means applied directly to the encoded Likert items — which is exactly
  the "12 raw items (naive approach)" row we benchmark in §4.2 and reject in §2.4, because
  clustering un-reduced items sorts respondents by *how high they answered overall* rather than by
  pattern. Their split is close to ours in proportion (241 / 210 / 159 against our 361 / 376 / 250),
  but we do **not** read that as support for k = 3: the published description documents no
  selection rule for their k, and our own k = 2 candidate produced a comparably plausible-looking
  split before we rejected it on shape (§3.3). External support for k = 3 comes from §0.3 instead.

### 0.3 Cao & Zhang (2026) — stress profiles in engineering undergraduates

> Cao, X., & Zhang, Y. (2026). *Academic stress and depression among engineering undergraduates:
> a latent profile and moderated mediation analysis.* **Current Psychology, 45**(8), 791.
> doi:10.1007/s12144-026-09349-2

A latent profile analysis of **1,455 undergraduate engineering students**, which recovered
**three** academic-stress profiles: low research stress (62.0%), low academic stress (17.4%) and
high academic stress (20.6%).

**What we took from it:** the best available external check on our choice of k. It reaches three
groups in the same population we sampled — engineering undergraduates — using a different
instrument, a different country, and a model-based method (LPA) rather than distance-based
clustering. Our k = 3 is therefore not an artefact of the selection rule in §3.3. Its profiles are
far more lopsided than ours (62 / 17 / 21 vs our 37 / 38 / 25), which is what we would expect from
a study that profiles *severity* where we deliberately profile *pattern*.

### 0.4 Valles-Coral et al. (2026) — unsupervised profiles, then supervised explanation

> Valles-Coral, M. A., Injante, R., Pinedo, L., Juárez-Díaz, J. R., Torres-Delgado, W., Lévano, D.,
> Saavedra-Saavedra, J. A., García-Rivas-Plata, C., Gómez-Apaza, R. D., & García-Paredes, M.
> (2026). *Identification of latent profiles and determining factors of academic stress in
> university students: an integrated unsupervised–supervised machine learning approach.*
> **Data, 11**(6), 129. doi:10.3390/data11060129

605 students at Universidad Nacional de San Martín (Peru), measured with five validated
instruments (PSS-10, LASSI, MBI-SS, PSQI, A-CEA). Dimensionality reduction followed by clustering
produced **four latent profiles**; eight supervised regression models were then fitted to explain
them.

**What we took from it:** the closest published match to our design — reduce, cluster, then use a
supervised model to say what the clusters mean. We follow the same two stages (§2.5 → §3.1 → §4.4)
and differ at the second: they fit regressions to *predict* a stress score, whereas we fit a
decision tree to *describe* the clusters as readable rules, and we state explicitly that its
accuracy measures interpretability rather than prediction. Their use of five separate validated
instruments — rather than one pooled academic-stress scale — independently supports our §2.4
finding that this construct does not reduce to a single reliable dimension. Their k = 4 against our
k = 3 is taken up in Limitation 3.

### 0.5 Hennig (2007) — how stable a cluster must be before it can be trusted

> Hennig, C. (2007). *Cluster-wise assessment of cluster stability.* **Computational Statistics &
> Data Analysis, 52**(1), 258–271. doi:10.1016/j.csda.2006.11.025

Proposes non-parametric bootstrap resampling with the Jaccard coefficient as a per-cluster
stability measure, and publishes thresholds for reading it: a mean Jaccard of **≥ 0.75** marks a
valid, stable cluster; **0.6–0.75** indicates a real pattern whose exact membership is doubtful;
**below 0.6** the cluster should not be trusted.

**What we took from it:** the criterion itself, and a published bar to report against instead of an
adjective. Our bootstrap stability of **0.811** (§5.3) clears the 0.75 threshold. We also adopt his
caveat — that stability is not sufficient for validity, because an inflexible method can be stable
and still wrong — which is why stability is one of eight criteria in §3.3 rather than the deciding
one.

### 0.6 Haslam, Holland & Kuppens (2012) — most psychological constructs are continua

> Haslam, N., Holland, E., & Kuppens, P. (2012). *Categories versus dimensions in personality and
> psychopathology: a quantitative review of taxometric research.* **Psychological Medicine,
> 42**(5), 903–920. doi:10.1017/S0033291711001966

A quantitative review of 177 taxometric articles, 311 findings and 533,377 participants. Only about
**14%** of constructs showed genuinely categorical (taxonic) structure once methodological
confounds were controlled; the broad neurotic spectrum — depression, generalised anxiety, stress
reactions — came out **dimensional**.

**What we took from it:** the correct reading of our own weakest number. Our gap statistic prefers
k = 1 and our silhouette is 0.139. Before this paper we treated that as a failure to be confessed;
it is in fact the expected result. A stress construct *should* come out dimensional, and a method
that manufactured cleanly separated stress populations from data like ours would be the outcome to
distrust. This is why §5.3 and §7.1 describe the personas as a defensible segmentation of a
continuum, and why we treat the *reproducibility* of the cut, rather than its depth, as the finding
worth reporting.

### 0.7 How our work differs

| | Prior work | This project |
|---|---|---|
| Language | Monolingual instruments (English, Spanish, Chinese) | **Bilingual** English + Bangla, with romanised Bangla in free text |
| Instrument | Validated multi-scale batteries (PSS-10, MBI-SS, …) or a fixed item pool | A 12-item pool written for this population, whose dimensionality we **test** rather than assume (§2.4) |
| How groups arise | Model-based LPA, or k-means straight onto the raw items | Clustering on **factor-derived subscales**, with k fixed by a rule written down **before** the run (§2.5, §3.3) |
| Role of the supervised model | Predicts a stress score | **Describes** the clusters as rules — and we say plainly that this measures interpretability, not prediction (§4.4) |
| Open-ended data | Not used | Two free-text questions, analysed with a purpose-built trilingual lexicon (§6) |
| Reproducibility | Single tool, single run | Held-out variables, a train/test split, and a full replication in a second tool (§4.3) |
| Negative results | Not reported | Criteria disagreement (§3.3), a null free-text result (§6.3) and the continuum finding (§5.3) all reported |

## 2. Data preprocessing techniques

Seven steps, applied in this order.

### 2.1 Schema validation

The Google Form export uses the full bilingual question text as column headers, so columns are
read by position. Each position is checked against a keyword ("breakfast", "pile", "sleep", …) and
the program **stops** if a column does not match. A pipeline that analyses the wrong columns and
produces believable numbers is worse than one that refuses to run.

### 2.2 Reverse coding

`AskTeacher` and `Feedback` are positively worded — agreeing with them means *less* stress. Both
were recoded as **6 − x** so that a high score always means more strain. Without this step these
two items correlate negatively with everything else and the analysis is meaningless.

### 2.3 Standardisation

All items share a 1–5 scale, so scaling looks unnecessary. It is not: item standard deviations
range from **0.98 to 1.46**. Left unscaled, the two most spread-out items (CGPA comparison and
financial concern) would dominate every distance calculation. We applied **z-score
standardisation** (mean 0, SD 1).

> In WEKA this is the `Standardize` filter — **not** `Normalize`. `Normalize` rescales by observed
> range, which lets a single extreme respondent set the scale.

### 2.4 Reliability check

Before clustering we tested whether the twelve items measure one thing:

| Statistic | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Cronbach's α | **0.609** | Below the 0.70 threshold — not one reliable scale |
| KMO | 0.669 | Adequate for factor analysis |
| Mean inter-item correlation | 0.123 | Items are largely independent |

**This changed the whole approach.** Because the items do not form a single scale, a combined
"stress score" is not valid, and clustering the twelve raw items measures only *how high someone
answered overall* — that is, severity, not pattern.

This matches how the construct is handled elsewhere. Valles-Coral et al. (§0.4) do not use a single
academic-stress scale either, but five separate validated instruments covering perceived stress,
study strategies, burnout, sleep quality and exam anxiety. A low α across twelve pooled items is
evidence that the pool **spans several dimensions**, not that the items are bad.

### 2.5 Factor analysis and subscale construction

Exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation found **four clean factors** with no
cross-loading items:

| Factor | Items | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation | ExamWorry, ResultDemotiv, CGPACompare | Exam and performance anxiety |
| Workload | PileUp, MissMeal, SleepLoss, LabStress | Workload and self-care sacrifice |
| FutureMacro | JobWorry, SocioPol | Career and wider insecurity |
| SupportGap | AskTeacher, Feedback | Gap in teacher support |

We averaged each factor's items into a **subscale score**, and separated **Financial** into its own
subscale because it is the most divided item in the survey (49% agree, 31% disagree) and central to
our research question. This gives **five stress dimensions** instead of twelve noisy ones.

### 2.6 Adding CGPA

The CGPA band was added as an ordinal feature (1 = below 2.50 … 5 = 3.80–4.00), because a "persona"
combines an achievement level with a stress pattern. The cost is stated openly: CGPA can no longer
be used to validate the clusters, so **year, gender, living arrangement, department and backlog
were all held out** for that purpose instead.

**Backlog was deliberately excluded as a feature.** At 6.4% prevalence it immediately forms its own
63-student cluster and takes over the result — we confirmed this by accident in WEKA (§4.5).

### 2.7 Train / test split

A **70/30 split** (690 train, 297 test), stratified by overall stress level. All model selection
used the training set only; the held-out 30% was used once, at the end, to check the clusters
reproduce.

**Final feature set: 6 columns × 987 rows** — Evaluation, Workload, FutureMacro, SupportGap,
Financial, CGPA_ord.

## 3. Models used

### 3.1 Clustering models

| Model | Purpose | Settings |
|---|---|---|
| **k-means** | Main clustering model | k = 2…8, k-means++ init, 25 restarts |
| **Expectation-Maximisation (EM)** | Probabilistic alternative; can choose k itself | Gaussian mixture, full and diagonal covariance |
| **Hierarchical (Ward)** | Shows the merge structure as a dendrogram | Ward, complete and average linkage |
| **Spectral clustering** | Handles non-spherical shapes | k = 2…8 |

Each model was run for every k from 2 to 8, giving **245 model fits** in total.

### 3.2 Supporting models

Five further models support the clustering rather than performing it. **PCA** provides
dimensionality reduction and the 2-D visualisation; **exploratory factor analysis** builds the
subscales (§2.5); a **decision tree** (J48 in WEKA, CART in the notebook) expresses each persona as
readable if-then rules; a **random forest** ranks which dimension separates the personas most; and
a **TF-IDF + NMF** topic model serves as a cross-check on the free text (§6).

### 3.3 Choosing the number of clusters

We did not pick k by eye. Eight criteria were computed and the rule was **written down before the
run**:

- **Elbow** (within-cluster sum of squares), **Silhouette**, **Davies–Bouldin**,
  **Calinski–Harabasz**, **Gap statistic**, **GMM BIC**, **Bootstrap stability**, and a
  **shape-vs-level measure** that checks whether clusters differ in *pattern* or only in *severity*.

The criteria disagreed — which is itself a finding, and we report it rather than hiding it:

| Criterion | Chose k = |
|---|---|
| Elbow | 4 |
| Silhouette | 2 |
| Calinski–Harabasz | 2 |
| Bootstrap stability | 2 |
| Davies–Bouldin | 8 |
| GMM BIC | 8 |
| Shape-vs-level | 8 |
| Gap statistic | **1** |

Candidate values of k were then screened for usability — smallest cluster at least 5% of the
sample, reproducible under resampling, differing in shape rather than only severity, and at most 6
clusters so the result stays actionable. **k = 2 was rejected** because only 41% of its
between-cluster variation is shape: it is a high-stress / low-stress split, not a set of personas.
Among the survivors (k = 3, 4, 6), **k = 3** scored best on average rank.

**An external check.** Cao & Zhang (§0.3) ran latent profile analysis on 1,455 engineering
undergraduates and also recovered three academic-stress profiles. Their method (model-based LPA),
instrument and country all differ from ours, so this is independent of our selection rule — as is
WEKA's EM arriving at k = 3 on its own by cross-validated log-likelihood (§4.3).

## 4. Comparison between results from different models

Everything below was run **twice** — once in Python (notebook) and once in WEKA — to check the
result is a property of the data and not of one tool.

### 4.1 Clustering algorithms compared (k = 3, final feature set)

| Model | Silhouette ↑ | Davies–Bouldin ↓ | Calinski–Harabasz ↑ | Smallest cluster |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **k-means** | **0.139** | **1.97** | **120.8** | 24.8% |
| Spectral | 0.118 | 1.99 | 110.0 | 30.9% |
| EM (diagonal) | 0.100 | 2.28 | 96.2 | 28.8% |
| EM (full) | 0.099 | 2.25 | 91.6 | 29.4% |
| Ward | 0.074 | 2.30 | 81.5 | 24.9% |
| Complete linkage | 0.110 | 2.09 | 42.4 | 1.6% ✗ |
| Average linkage | 0.400 | 0.76 | 10.2 | 0.1% ✗ |

**k-means wins** on all three balanced measures and was selected as the final model.

> **Why average linkage was rejected despite the best silhouette.** It puts 99% of students in one
> cluster and hangs a handful of outliers off it. A high score with a 0.1% smallest cluster
> segments nothing. Any linkage leaving over 80% of students in one cluster was automatically
> excluded, and the reason recorded.

### 4.2 Feature sets compared (k-means, k = 3)

This is the comparison that justifies the preprocessing in §2.5.

| Feature set | Silhouette | Davies–Bouldin | Calinski–Harabasz |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 raw items *(naive approach — see §0.2)* | 0.124 | 2.47 | 79.2 |
| 4 subscales | **0.183** | **1.60** | **181.9** |
| 5 subscales | 0.149 | 1.88 | 140.7 |
| 4 subscales + CGPA | 0.155 | 1.83 | 140.4 |
| **5 subscales + CGPA** *(chosen)* | 0.139 | 1.97 | 120.8 |

Moving from raw items to subscales improves every measure. The final set trades a little
separation for interpretability — it is the only one that produces personas describing achievement
*and* stress pattern together, which is what the project set out to find.

The top row is not a straw man we built to knock down: it is the method behind the published
`Stress_Class` label in Hussen et al. (§0.2), whose three classes were produced by k-means on the
encoded items. Running that approach on our own data costs 0.06 silhouette against the best
subscale set, and well under half its Calinski–Harabasz.

### 4.3 Python vs WEKA

The same models were re-run in WEKA 3.8 on an exported ARFF file. Agreement is measured with the
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI): 1.0 = identical grouping, 0 = chance.

| WEKA model | Agreement with notebook | ARI |
|---|---|---|
| **k-means** | **76.2%** | **0.459** |
| Ward | 62.3% | 0.215 |
| EM | 56.6% | 0.109 |

Two results stand out:

1. **EM chose k = 3 on its own.** Given `-N -1`, WEKA's EM picks the cluster count by
   cross-validated log-likelihood — a completely different criterion from ours — and returned
   **three clusters**. This is our strongest independent confirmation.
2. **k-means centroids match closely.** WEKA's cluster centres reproduce our profile shapes
   dimension by dimension, including CGPA as the strongest separator.

WEKA does **not** reproduce the exact same partition, and we do not claim it does. Our own six
algorithms agree with each other at an average ARI of only **0.339**, so WEKA's spread of
0.109–0.459 is the expected range. The disagreement confirms our main finding: the groups are a
segmentation of a continuum, and where exactly the boundaries fall depends on the algorithm.

### 4.4 Decision tree — describing the personas as rules

| | Notebook (CART) | WEKA (J48) |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy (10-fold CV) | 76.8% ± 4.1% | **80.5%** |
| Kappa | — | 0.703 |
| Leaves | 16 | 17 |
| Top splits | CGPA → Financial → FutureMacro | **CGPA → Financial → FutureMacro** |

Both tools produce the same variable ordering, matching the Random Forest importance ranking
(CGPA 0.275, Financial 0.190, FutureMacro 0.170).

> **What this measures.** The tree is trained on labels the clustering produced, so its accuracy
> shows *how cleanly the personas can be described* — not how well anything predicts stress. It is
> interpretability, not prediction.

### 4.5 A useful mistake

While setting up WEKA we accidentally left `backlog` in as a clustering feature. k-means
immediately created a cluster of exactly **63 students — precisely the 63 with a backlog**. This is
why backlog is held out (§2.6), demonstrated rather than argued. The run is kept as
`weka_runs/90_backlog_hijack_demo.txt`.

## 5. Results — the three personas

### 5.1 Profiles

Values are z-scores: positive = above average, negative = below average.

| Persona | n | % | Evaluation | Workload | Future | Support gap | Financial | CGPA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 — Struggling & financially strained** | 361 | 36.6% | +0.26 | +0.46 | +0.17 | **+0.47** | **+0.67** | **−0.70** |
| **P2 — High-achieving & future-anxious** | 376 | 38.1% | +0.18 | +0.12 | **+0.46** | **−0.38** | −0.12 | **+0.68** |
| **P3 — Low-strain & coping** | 250 | 25.3% | −0.65 | **−0.84** | **−0.94** | −0.10 | **−0.78** | −0.02 |

**In plain words:**

- **P1** — lower CGPA, money worries, heavy workload, and least likely to get help from teachers.
  12.2% have a backlog, roughly double the sample rate.
- **P2** — highest CGPA band (average 4.30) and good relations with teachers, but the most anxious
  about jobs and the future. Only 1.3% have a backlog.
- **P3** — below average on every stressor. The comparison group.

### 5.2 What separates them

| Dimension | η² (share of variance explained) |
|---|---|
| CGPA | 0.355 |
| Financial | 0.323 |
| Future / macro | 0.314 |
| Workload | 0.259 |
| Evaluation | 0.142 |
| Support gap | 0.138 |

### 5.3 Are the groups real?

We report this honestly.

| Check | Result | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Silhouette | 0.139 | Weak separation |
| Gap statistic | k = 1 | No naturally separated groups |
| Bootstrap stability | 0.811 | Clears Hennig's 0.75 bar for a valid, stable cluster |
| Seed stability | 0.933 | Same result across 20 random starts |
| Holdout test | Passed | Cluster sizes and centres reproduce on unseen 30% |

These are not contradictory, and the literature tells us which way to read each one.

**On the weak separation.** Haslam et al. (§0.6) reviewed 311 taxometric findings across 533,377
participants and found that only about 14% of psychological constructs are genuinely categorical;
the stress and anxiety spectrum is dimensional. A gap statistic of k = 1 on a stress instrument is
therefore the *expected* result, not a failed analysis — and a method that returned cleanly
separated stress populations from data like ours would be the result to distrust.

**On the stability.** Hennig (§0.5) gives a published bar for bootstrap Jaccard stability: ≥ 0.75
is a valid, stable cluster, 0.6–0.75 means the pattern is real but membership is doubtful, and
below 0.6 the cluster should not be trusted. Our **0.811** sits in the top band. Following his
caveat that stability alone does not establish validity, we do not rest the case on it: it is one
of eight criteria (§3.3), corroborated by seed stability, the holdout test, and two independent
recoveries of k = 3 (§3.3, §4.3).

Student stress is a **continuum**, and k-means reliably draws the *same cut* through it every time.
The cut is reproducible; it is not a natural gap. We therefore describe the output as **"a
defensible segmentation of a continuum"** rather than as discovered groups — which, on the
taxometric evidence, is the strongest claim this construct supports.

### 5.4 The personas are not demographics in disguise

None of the five held-out variables explains the personas (Cramér's V ≤ 0.19, all effect sizes
small):

| Variable | Cramér's V | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Backlog | 0.188 | Small |
| Living arrangement | 0.106 | Small |
| Academic year | 0.104 | Small |
| Gender | 0.103 | Small |
| Department | 0.082 | Negligible |

WEKA reproduced this independently: backlog V = 0.163, academic year ARI = 0.0099.

## 6. Free-text analysis

Students wrote very short answers (median **5 words**, 28% two words or fewer) mixing **English,
Bangla script and romanised Bangla**. This ruled out standard NLP: sentiment analysis has no
variance (the question asks for a stress source), and an English-only pipeline would silently drop
answers.

We built a **13-theme keyword lexicon** covering all three writing systems. It tagged **85.7%** of
answers. A TF-IDF topic model was run as a cross-check and **failed on 100% of Bangla-script
answers**, which is exactly the evidence for why the lexicon was needed.

### 6.1 Most common themes

| Theme | % of answers |
|---|---|
| Lab & coursework load | 28.5 |
| Exams & results | 24.3 |
| General academic pressure | 14.3 |
| Career & job uncertainty | 13.8 |
| Time & schedule pressure | 11.8 |

### 6.2 Stressors the survey never asked about

The most useful output for the department — things students raised unprompted that none of the
twelve items covered:

Cannot follow lectures · Viva examinations · Non-departmental courses · Session jam ·
Adjusting to a new city · Preparatory leave too short · Homesickness · Hall/mess food ·
Campus politics · Cost of models and drawings · Transport · English-language weakness

### 6.3 An honest negative result

We tested whether the free-text themes differ between the three personas. **None of the 13 themes
was significant** after correcting for multiple tests. The financial theme leaned in the expected
direction (10.0% / 6.6% / 4.2%) but not enough to count. We report this as a null result.

---

## 7. Limitations

1. **The groups are not naturally separated.** Silhouette ≈ 0.14 and the gap statistic prefers a
   single group: they are a useful segmentation, not three distinct populations. We now read this
   as a property of the construct rather than a fault in the pipeline (Haslam et al., §0.6), but
   the practical consequence is unchanged — no student should be treated as *belonging* to a
   persona, and borderline cases are genuinely borderline.
2. **CGPA does much of the work.** It is the strongest separator by design, so the personas are
   partly an achievement split with a stress pattern attached.
3. **k = 3 and k = 4 were nearly tied** (mean rank 1.86 vs 2.00). Both are defensible; k = 4 is
   reported as the runner-up. The published work splits the same way — Cao & Zhang report three
   profiles, Valles-Coral et al. four (§0.3–0.4) — which suggests the tie reflects the construct
   itself rather than anything peculiar to our data.
4. **Some subscales are weak.** `FutureMacro` combines only two items with low internal
   consistency (α = 0.26) and is effectively driven by the job-worry item alone.
5. **The free text did not corroborate the personas** (§6.3).
6. **Sample imbalance** — 79% male, 64% in years 1–2, three departments supply most responses.
7. **Self-reported, cross-sectional, single institution** — no causal claims are possible.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Our main contribution is the **diagnosis** as much as the personas. The twelve stress items do not
form a single scale (α = 0.61), so clustering them directly only measures who answered high overall.
Reducing them to interpretable subscales through factor analysis changes the question from *how
stressed* to *stressed in what way*, and that is what produced three usable personas instead of a
high/low split.

Four algorithms in two tools agree on the profile shapes; WEKA's EM independently chose the same
number of clusters, as did a published latent profile analysis of 1,455 engineering undergraduates
(§0.3); a decision tree describes each persona in readable rules with ~80% accuracy; and five
held-out variables confirm the personas are not simply demographics.

That the personas sit on a continuum rather than in natural gaps is not a shortcoming of the
method — it is what the taxometric literature predicts for a construct of this kind (§0.6), and our
bootstrap stability of 0.811 clears the published threshold for a cluster worth trusting (§0.5).

For the department, the practical outputs are the three personas — each with a suggested
intervention — and the twelve stressors students volunteered that the questionnaire never thought
to ask about.

## 10. How to reproduce

### 10.1 Python

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python src/stress_personas.py          # ~8 minutes; writes everything into outputs/
```

Or open `notebooks/stress_personas.ipynb` and Run All — it contains the same code and gives
identical numbers. Everything is seeded (`random_state = 42`), so results are exactly repeatable.

### 10.2 WEKA

Load `outputs/stress_prepared.arff` and apply the `Standardize` filter, then run
`SimpleKMeans -N 3 -init 1 -I 500 -S 9` for k-means, `EM -I 100 -N -1 -X 10 -S 42` for EM with
automatic k, `HierarchicalClusterer -N 3 -L WARD -P` for Ward, and `J48 -C 0.25 -M 20` with
class = `cluster` under 10-fold cross-validation for the decision tree.

For validation against held-out variables use `outputs/validate_backlog.arff` (and the matching
files for gender, year, living, department) with **Classes to clusters evaluation**.

Detailed step-by-step instructions: [`docs/WEKA_APPENDIX.md`](docs/WEKA_APPENDIX.md).
Saved run outputs and results: [`weka_runs/README.md`](weka_runs/README.md).

### 10.3 Outputs

The run writes 30 diagnostic figures to `outputs/figures/` and 31 CSV tables to `outputs/tables/`;
the twelve figures reproduced in this report are the curated subset in `outputs/report_figures/`.
Every number cited here is in `outputs/results.json`, per-student persona assignments are in
`outputs/student_assignments.csv`, and `outputs/models/` holds the saved model, reusable on next
semester's responses.
